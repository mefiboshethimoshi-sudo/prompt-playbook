prompt 3: explain the difference between what you ‘knows’ vs what you ‘thinks’ 
AI RESPONSE
The difference between what I **“know”** and what I **“think”** comes down to how I generate responses:
* **What I “know”:**
  This refers to information learned during training from a large amount of text data. It includes patterns, facts, language structures, and general knowledge up to my training cutoff. I don’t store facts like a human memory, but I can reproduce information that matches what I was trained on.

* **What I “think”:**
  This refers to how I generate a response in the moment. I don’t have beliefs or opinions like a human. Instead, I predict the most likely and relevant words based on your input and the context of the conversation. This process is sometimes described as “reasoning” or “inference.”

**In short:**
* “Know” = learned information from training data
* “Think” = generated response based on patterns and context, not personal beliefs or consciousness





prompt 4:what types of tasks you are genuinely bad at 
AI RESPONSE
There are several types of tasks I’m genuinely not good at or not reliable for:

* **Real-time or very recent information**: I may not know the latest news, updates, or live data unless additional tools are used.
* **Highly precise factual verification**: I can make mistakes or give outdated/inaccurate details, especially for niche or rapidly changing topics.
* **Personal experiences or emotions**: I don’t have real feelings, memories, or lived experiences.
* **Physical-world actions**: I can’t perform tasks in the real world (e.g., making calls, moving objects, visiting places).
* **Complex calculations without tools**: I can explain math, but I may make errors in long or very detailed computations.
* **Highly specialized expert decisions**: In areas like medical, legal, or financial advice, I can provide general guidance but not definitive professional judgment.

In general, I’m best at explaining concepts, generating text, helping with ideas, and assisting with structured problems—but less reliable for things that require real-time data, physical presence, or guaranteed precision.


prompt 5: describe you are own uncertainty on a topic
AI RESPONSE
I don’t have personal feelings like uncertainty, but I can represent **confidence levels** in my responses based on how reliable the underlying information is.

When I’m **more certain**, it’s usually because:

* The topic is common and well-established (e.g., basic math, general concepts)
* The information is consistent across many sources
* The question is clear and unambiguous

When I’m **less certain**, it’s often because:

* The topic is very niche, new, or rapidly changing
* The question is ambiguous or missing key details
* There are conflicting sources or multiple possible interpretations
* It involves predictions or subjective opinions

In those cases, I may:

* Provide a cautious answer
* Include alternatives or possibilities
* Or ask clarifying questions before giving a more precise response

So instead of “feeling uncertain,” I estimate uncertainty by how much reliable information and clarity I have for the task.

AI systems do not possess true self-awareness or consciousness like humans. While they can describe concepts of awareness, this is a simulation of language rather than genuine understanding or self-awareness.
